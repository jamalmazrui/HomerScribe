WHAT I FOUND, AND WHAT I DID NOT

You asked for TV on YouTube that is legal either because the publisher put it

there or because copyright has expired. Those two routes turn out to be very

different in practice.

The publisher route works. The expired-copyright route, on YouTube, mostly

does not – not because such shows do not exist, but because the uploads are

not what they appear to be. I opened two of the best-known playlists:

“The Andy Griffith Show Full Episodes” – owner “Orio”, 143 videos. Only

about thirty episodes of that series are in the public domain, all from the

1962-63 season. A playlist of 143 is therefore mostly not.

“You Bet Your Life” – owner “Rick Nineg”, one video.

Neither is a publisher, and neither playlist matches what is actually out of

copyright. I have not listed them. Genuinely public-domain television is

better found on the Internet Archive, where the status is stated on the page

and the files download directly.

NOVA – a television series, published by its own broadcaster

—————————————————————————

NOVA has run on PBS since 1974. Its own channel publishes complete episodes,

not clips, which makes it the cleanest answer to your question: a TV show, on

YouTube, put there by the people who made it.

Full Documentaries, NOVA PBS Official, 106 videos, roughly an hour each.

HomerScribe will warn about the size and then begin at the first episode.

https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLtiOgIRVt4053SXtk0cVIIkhFfbfkac0e

To describe particular episodes instead, open that playlist, copy the

addresses of the ones you want, put them on lines of their own below, and put

a # in front of the playlist address above.

There is a second reason to choose NOVA. PBS publishes professionally written

and performed audio description for many NOVA and NATURE programmes on

pbs.org. So you can describe one episode with HomerScribe and listen to PBS’s

description of another, on the same series, in the same week. No other source

lets you do that.

What a TV show will actually test

—————————————————————————

Everything HomerScribe has been given so far has been at one extreme or the

other: documentaries at 78 to 86 percent speech, where there is no room for

description, or a silent film at nought, where there is nothing but room.

Drama is the case in between, and the one audio description was invented for.

A scripted programme runs about 30 to 40 percent speech: real pauses between

lines, and things happening in them that the dialogue does not account for.

The Odyssey run was the closest to this, and it produced the best placement

figures of any talking film – 15 percent of descriptions landing on speech,

against 86 percent on the documentaries.

So a NOVA episode will tell you how HomerScribe handles a factual programme.

A scripted drama would tell you something more interesting still, and if you

can find one that is lawfully available, that is the test I would most like to

see the results of.
